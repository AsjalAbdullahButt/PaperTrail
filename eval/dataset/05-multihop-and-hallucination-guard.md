# Multi-Hop Retrieval and the Hallucination Guard

## Why multi-hop retrieval exists

A single round of retrieval works well when the answer to a question lives entirely in one or two nearby passages. It works poorly when answering the question requires first discovering one fact, and only then knowing what second, related fact to search for. PaperTrail's multi-hop mode, implemented in `backend/app/services/multihop.py`, addresses this by chaining exactly two retrieval rounds together rather than relying on a single query.

## How multi-hop retrieval works

The first round calls `hybrid_retrieve` with the user's original question, asking for the top 5 results. The system then hands the original question and a short preview of what that first round found (the first 200 characters of up to 5 retrieved chunks) to the language model, along with an instruction to write exactly one more specific search query that would surface additional relevant information — this refined query is generated by `complete_text`, a single-turn auxiliary completion helper, not by the main answer-generation path. A second `hybrid_retrieve` call is then issued using this refined query (falling back to the original question if refinement produced nothing useful), again asking for the top 5 results.

The results of both rounds are merged into one dictionary keyed by chunk id: when the same chunk appears in both rounds, PaperTrail keeps whichever round's `ranked_score` was higher rather than double-counting or averaging. The merged results are then sorted by that ranked score, descending, and truncated to the top 8. If any results survive, the system records that all of those chunks were retrieved (the same coverage-tracking side effect that a single-round `hybrid_retrieve` call has), so multi-hop queries still contribute to the coverage heatmap and analytics.

## The hallucination guard

After an answer is generated in RAG mode, PaperTrail runs a second, independent check called the hallucination guard, implemented in `backend/app/services/hallucination_guard.py`. Its job is to flag sentences in the generated answer that are not actually supported by any of the retrieved chunks — a defense against the model inventing a plausible-sounding but ungrounded claim.

The guard is deliberately lexical rather than another model call: adding a second LLM call per answer would double generation latency and cost, and a purely lexical check, while less nuanced, is fast, free, and conservative. It works by splitting the generated answer into sentences, then for each sentence extracting its "key terms" — a lexical proxy for claim-bearing content, consisting of capitalized words longer than two characters (excluding common sentence-initial words like "The," "This," "However," and similar function words that happen to start a sentence), numeric tokens (including ones with commas, decimals, or a trailing percent sign), and other words of five or more letters (excluding a short list of common non-content words like "which," "would," and "about").

A sentence is marked as **supported** if it shares at least three key terms with at least one of the retrieved chunks; the chunk contributing the match is recorded as that sentence's `source_chunk_id`. A sentence containing no checkable key terms at all — for example, a purely transitional sentence with no proper nouns, numbers, or long content words — is not flagged, on the theory that there is nothing in it to verify in the first place. The output is a list of `{"sentence", "supported", "source_chunk_id"}` records, one per sentence of the answer, which the frontend can use to visually mark unsupported claims for the user.

## Why the threshold is three shared terms, not one

Requiring only a single shared key term between a sentence and a chunk would produce many false "supported" verdicts purely from incidental word overlap — two unrelated sentences about the same general topic will often share one proper noun or one long word by coincidence. Requiring three shared key terms is a deliberately conservative bar intended to catch the clearly unsupported cases (a claim referencing an entity, number, or specific term found nowhere in the evidence) without needing true semantic entailment checking, which would require another model call.
